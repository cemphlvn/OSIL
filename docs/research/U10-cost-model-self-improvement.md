## TL;DR

**Do not build a learned cost model yet. Calibrate the two named physical constants directly, against measured ground truth, using the machinery `optimizer/bench/` already has — wrapped in a small append-only ledger and a held-out-by-kernel-family regression gate.** This is not a cop-out relative to the literature; it is what the literature's own stated preconditions imply. Halide's, AutoTVM's, and Ansor's authors each built (or adopted) a learned model *because* their search spaces reached 10⁵–10⁹ candidates and on-device measurement was the scarce resource the model exists to conserve. `optimizer/`'s space for the reduction/lanes problem is five candidates (`Chain` + `Lanes{4,i}` for `i ∈ {1,2,4,8}`, confirmed against `optimizer/src/main.rs::rules()`), and `optimizer/bench/Makefile` already builds and times all of them in one `make all` run. There is no measurement bottleneck here for a model to amortize. What *is* genuinely unvalidated — `MUL_LATENCY = 4.0`, `LANES_PER_CYCLE = 8.0` — is a two-scalar calibration problem with a closed-form, one-shot least-squares solution, not a training loop. The self-improvement the maintainer wants is real and worth building; it is a calibration ledger, not an ML pipeline.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the primary-source claims about Halide/AutoTVM/Ansor/TenSet/MLGO's own mechanisms and stated numbers (all read directly from paper text, cited with page-level attribution). HIGH on current maintenance status of the reuse candidates (live API queries, timestamped). MEDIUM-HIGH on the scale-mismatch argument (§5) — it is a judgment call, clearly flagged as such, not a measured fact; the counter-argument is given equal space in §5 rather than suppressed.

## 1. Halide autoscheduler lineage

### 1.1 Mullapudi et al. 2016 — the analytical model being replaced

Per Adams et al. 2019 §2 (Related Work), in the 2019 authors' own words:

> "The current autoscheduler shipped as part of Halide is derived from Mullapudi et al.'s work [2016]. It performs greedy search over the possible applications of a simple schedule template using a simple cost model. This allows it to run quickly, with no benchmarking at all, but it only considers a single level of tiling and fusion, combined with fixed heuristic choices for things like parallelism, vectorization, and unrolling. This excludes many useful schedules (such as multi-level tiling and line-buffering), and has proven very difficult to generalize across applications or to new schedules."

Mechanically (Adams et al. §2, and consistent with the paper title "Automatically Scheduling Halide Image Processing Pipelines"): an *analytical, closed-form* heuristic — overlapping-tile analysis plus a greedy stage-grouping/merge algorithm, estimating arithmetic cost per stage to size tiles that minimize data loads. No machine learning, no benchmarking during search. This is architecturally the closest published analogue to what `optimizer/`'s `model()` is today: a hand-derived formula over a small number of named quantities, evaluated at compile time with zero on-device measurement.

**Its own field's verdict on it, from the people who built its successor:** analytically sound but *"difficult to generalize"* — not because the formula was wrong in kind, but because it was hand-tuned for the cases its authors had in mind, and the number of interacting scheduling primitives it needed to cover kept growing. This is the precondition that justified building a learned model — see §5.

### 1.2 Adams et al. 2019 — the learned cost model that replaced it

**Representation (read directly, §4.1–4.2, Figure 4):** *not* an end-to-end black-box network over raw code. A **hybrid**: 39 hand-designed, schedule-dependent numerical features per pipeline stage (log-transformed, since they "have a large dynamic range and are most naturally combined multiplicatively") plus a 40-bucket histogram of algorithm operation types, each fed through a small feed-forward network (two heads — schedule-embedding, algorithm-embedding — concatenated, 32-wide hidden layer) that outputs **27 non-negative coefficients**, one per hand-designed cost *term* (e.g., "bytes read that were written on another core," "number of page faults accounting for thread serialization"). The final predicted cost is the **dot product of hand-designed terms with learned coefficients** — the network decides how much each term matters, not what the terms are. Quoting directly: *"Attempting to predict the runtime of Halide programs directly using a neural network requires a large network and is hard to train... Instead, our cost model outputs runtime as a dot product between a vector of hand-designed terms and a vector of coefficients predicted by a small neural network."* This is directly relevant to `optimizer/`: its own `model()` is already exactly this shape minus the learned part — two named terms (`latency`, `throughput`) combined by a hand-written formula (`max` + `combine`), with two scalar coefficients. Adams et al.'s own architectural choice validates keeping the *formula* hand-written and putting the learning/calibration surface only on the *coefficients* — which is precisely what §5's recommendation below does, just with a closed-form fit instead of gradient descent, because there are 2 coefficients here, not 27.

**Training data generation (§5):** a random DAG-structured program generator drawing from a library of stage types common in imaging/deep-learning code, **synthesizing algorithms that "did not need to be long, or even meaningful,"** because the model only needs to reason locally about a stage's immediate context. Batches of 32 schedules per random algorithm; iterative bootstrapping — five rounds, each round "ten thousand random algorithms times thirty-two random schedules," retraining after each round on the schedules the *current* model's search is likely to generate (an on-policy correction, not fixed offline data). Distributed across a cluster: Xeon servers for x86 weights, an NVIDIA V100 cluster for GPU weights.

**Overfitting avoidance (§6.1, verbatim):** the strongest and most directly reusable finding in this whole survey. Training data is **synthetic random programs**; evaluation uses **two structurally disjoint sets** — "unseen random pipelines" (same *distribution* as training, but never-seen instances) and a **curated benchmark suite of real applications** (`stencil_chain`, `camera_pipe`, `resnet50`, `bilateral_grid`, etc.) that the model never sees in any form. Quoting §6.1 directly: *"We also evaluate the system on a benchmark suite of real applications to ensure we do not overfit to randomly-generated pipelines... We found no benefit from autotuning on the random pipelines. While these random pipelines were not part of the training set, they are samples from the same distribution, and so the model can predict runtime accurately."* This is train/test separation by **construction of two different generating processes**, not merely held-out indices from one dataset — a stronger discipline than a random split, and the one worth copying.

**Measured benefit over Mullapudi 2016 ("Halide master," Fig. 6/8, §6):** greedy mode 50% faster than master; beam search 75% faster; beam search + autotuning (a modest sampling/benchmarking budget) 95% faster; beam search + autotuning + iterative retraining on the specific application 135% faster on average. On random unseen pipelines: greedy 1.37× geomean, beam search 2.29× geomean over master.

**Stated limitations, §7, the authors' own words, ranked above my inference per this project's evidence hierarchy:**
- *"Expanding the training set"* — real-application samples are not yet fed back into training; proposed as future work, not solved.
- *"Expanding the search space"* — new scheduling primitives require new hand-crafted features; *"investigating slow schedules and crafting new features that capture what makes them slow is difficult."* The model does not discover its own features.
- *"Reasoning in terms of constants"* — the cost model requires concrete output sizes; fully symbolic/dynamic shapes are handled by picking a representative middle iteration, which the authors flag can be "unusually cheap" for some algorithms — an acknowledged, unfixed source of error.
- **New-architecture bring-up does not transfer automatically.** For ARM: *"We lack enough ARM training samples to date to train network weights specifically for that architecture"* — yet the ARM-trained model, evaluated with weights *"fit to results from processors on the opposite end of the performance spectrum,"* still beat Halide master by only 9% (greedy) / 23% (beam) — markedly smaller margins than the 50%/75% seen on x86, i.e., **explicit, self-reported, partial-but-degraded transfer across architectures**, not silent success.

### 1.3 What this means for OSIL directly

`optimizer/`'s current constants are an M4 "ballpark," not measured — structurally the exact failure mode Adams et al. flag for their own under-sampled ARM model, one generation removed (they at least *trained* on some ARM data; OSIL's constants are not derived from any M4 measurement at all yet).

## 2. TVM: AutoTVM, Ansor, and TenSet

### 2.1 AutoTVM (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2018, "Learning to Optimize Tensor Programs")

**Cost model (§3.1):** two architectures compared head-to-head — **Gradient Boosted Trees (XGBoost)** over hand-extracted domain features (loop structure, memory-access counts, data-reuse ratio, plus generic annotations like vectorization/unrolling/thread-binding), versus a **TreeGRU** that recursively encodes the low-level AST into an embedding with no manual feature engineering. Direct quote on the tradeoff: *"GBT relies on precise feature extraction and makes fast predictions using CPUs... TreeGRU... is extensible and requires no feature engineering, but it lags in training and predictive speed."* GBT is the practical default.

**Training objective (§3.2, directly relevant to OSIL's gate design):** regression loss (MSE) is available, but the paper explicitly prefers a **pairwise rank loss** (Eq. 2: `Σ log(1 + e^{-sign(cᵢ-cⱼ)(f̂(xᵢ)-f̂(xⱼ))})`) because *"we care only about the relative order of program run times rather than their absolute values."* Fig. 5's caption states rank-based objectives "either outperformed or performed the same as the regression-based objective" across every workload tested.

**Avoiding overfitting to one kernel — transfer learning as the mechanism (§4):** AutoTVM's answer to "how do you not just memorize the training kernel" is architectural, not just a held-out split. They build a **representation invariant to the source/target workload** — "context relation features" for GBT (relations between loop-level feature axes, invariant across differently-shaped loop nests) or a "context-encoded TreeGRU" — so that `f̂(x) = f̂_global(x) + f̂_local(x)`: a model pretrained on many prior workloads (`𝒟'`) provides a good prior, and a small local model fine-tunes it fast for a genuinely new workload. Measured benefit of this specifically: **2×–10× faster convergence** than training a cost model from scratch on the new workload (§6.2, Fig. 8), and the invariant (context-relation) representation transfers even *across operator types* (conv → matmul), which flat/raw features do not (Fig. 9: "flattened AST features worked well when transferring across convolution workloads but were not useful across operator types; context relation representation allowed effective transfer across operator types").

**Measured benefit vs. baselines:** statistical cost model (GBT/TreeGRU) converges roughly 2× faster than black-box genetic search or random search on real hardware trials (Fig. 4); end-to-end, AutoTVM (no hand-tuned kernel library at all) is **1.2×–3.8×** faster than TensorFlow/MXNet/TFLite/ARM Compute Library across server GPU, embedded ARM Cortex-A53 CPU, and mobile ARM Mali GPU (§6.3, Fig. 11).

### 2.2 Ansor (Zheng et al., OSDI 2020, "Generating High-Performance Tensor Programs for Deep Learning")

**Cost model (§5.2):** a single global **gradient-boosted decision tree** (again, not a deep net — GBDT chosen explicitly for prediction speed), predicting per-innermost-non-loop-statement throughput scores from hand-extracted arithmetic + memory-access features, summed to a whole-program score. **Loss function is a weighted squared error, weighted by the measured throughput itself:** `loss(f,P,y) = w_p Σ(f(s)-y)²`, with `w_p = y` — i.e., *"we mostly care about identifying the well-performing programs... we put more weight on the programs that run faster."* This mirrors Adams et al.'s independent design choice (§1.2 above: minimize L2 error on *throughput*, not runtime, for the same stated reason) — **two unrelated groups converging on the identical design principle: weight the loss toward the fast candidates that actually matter for the final pick, not the whole dynamic range.**

**Retraining, not incremental fine-tuning:** *"a single model is trained for all tensor programs coming from all DAGs... we train a new model every time instead of doing incremental updates"* — because GBDT training on the small per-DNN measurement budget (<30,000 measured programs) is fast enough that there's no need for online/incremental training machinery. This is a second convergent point with §1.2's Halide bootstrapping loop: **both systems retrain from a growing measurement set rather than fine-tune weights in place** — a simpler, more auditable pattern than online gradient updates.

**Task scheduler (§6, separate from the cost model):** a gradient-descent-based bandit allocating one measurement-batch "time unit" per iteration to whichever subgraph/task has the largest estimated marginal improvement to the end-to-end objective, approximated from the task's own recent improvement rate and a similarity-adjusted comparison to related tasks (Eq. in §6.2). This solves "which of many tasks should I spend my next measurement on" — a real problem at Ansor's scale (dozens of subgraphs per DNN) that has no direct analogue in `optimizer/`'s scale (one kernel, one reduction) yet; noted for completeness, not recommended for adoption now.

**Ansor's own explicit limitations section:** not located in the pages read (through §6, Task Scheduler) — the paper appears to fold self-critique into comparison tables rather than a dedicated "Limitations" section. **ASSUMPTION-flagged:** I did not verify whether §7/§8 (not reached in this pass) contains one; treat "no self-reported Ansor limitation quoted here" as an evidentiary gap, not a claim that none exists.

**Exists, and is directly relevant to the task's specific question about ARM/CPU coverage.** Table 2 of the paper, read directly:

**ARM is present** — but it is AWS Graviton2, an **ARM Neoverse-N1 server core**, not Apple Silicon. Different microarchitecture, different NEON pipe count/latency, different clock, different cache hierarchy than the M4 `optimizer/` actually targets. **Direct implication: TenSet's ARM numbers cannot serve as ground truth for calibrating `optimizer/`'s M4 constants** — they are, at best, a sanity-check reference distribution for "is a NEON multiply-reduce roughly this shape," not a substitute for measuring the actual machine. Dataset scale: 51,577,248 measurement records, 120 networks × 6 platforms, 13,848 tasks (Table 1). Format is JSON measurement-record files with read/write utility functions provided.

**Currency (checked live, 2026-08-24, GitHub REST API against `tlc-pack/tenset`):** `pushed_at: 2022-11-04T10:26:56Z`, 101 stars, 16 open issues, not archived but **dormant roughly 4 years** as of this research. Usable as a static download (assuming the hosted files still resolve — not independently re-verified in this pass), but not an actively maintained project one could file issues against or expect fixes from.

**Train/held-out methodology (§5.1, the single most directly reusable finding in this survey):** *"We hold out a ResNet-50 (batch size=1, image size=224) as the test set, and use the rest of the dataset as the training set."* This is the **strong form** of held-out — an entire unseen network/kernel family excluded from training, not a random slice of schedules within an already-seen kernel. Table 4 evaluates on **five held-out networks by name** (ResNet-50, MobileNet-V2, ResNeXt-50, BERT-tiny, BERT-base) — i.e., the paper's own standard practice is multiple, named, structurally distinct held-out families, not one.

**Evaluation-metric finding, directly informs OSIL's gate design (§5.1, quoted):** *"According to Table 3, although Model #1 has a small RMSE and large R², it does not result in as good latency as Model #3, which has a high RMSE and an extremely low R²... RMSE and R² might not be the appropriate metrics for models trained with ranking losses... pairwise comparison accuracy and the top-k score are more consistent with the final latency... Intuitively, the problem we are tackling is fundamentally a ranking problem rather than a regression problem."* This is the **third** independent convergence (after Halide's throughput-weighted L2 and AutoTVM's rank loss) on the same underlying principle: **regression accuracy of the cost model is not the thing to optimize or gate on; whether the model's *pick* matches ground truth is.** `optimizer/README.md` already states this instinct correctly ("the harness checks the model's PICK against ground truth") — TenSet's §5.1 is direct, primary-sourced validation that this instinct is the field's own converged practice, not an ad hoc simplification.

**Cross-hardware transfer, authors' own finding (§5.4, Fig. 6b):** a cost model trained on one platform, applied to a different platform's search, *does* transfer and beat cold-start search — but *"the performance is not as good as on the platform that generates the dataset that the model is trained on."* This is the primary-sourced answer to the task's question about "a known result about learned cost models failing to transfer across hardware": **yes — explicit, quantified degradation under cross-hardware transfer, in the authors' own words**, not a failure so severe the model is useless, but a real, measured gap.

**Stated limitation (§7, verbatim):** *"the dataset only includes programs for floating point and dense neural networks. The subgraphs are partitioned by a specific algorithm, which limits the types of subgraphs."* The authors flag their own dataset as narrower than the field's real diversity even within its own domain.

## 3. MLGO (Google, LLVM production ML)

This is the section that actually resolves the task's determinism tension, so it is quoted at length; primary-sourced from Trofin et al. 2021, arXiv:2101.04808, read in full.

**Training loop and reward:** MLGO frames LLVM's inlining-for-size decision as a sequential MDP over the call graph and compares two training algorithms — Policy Gradient (via PPO) and Evolution Strategies. Reward for inlining-for-size (Eq. 1): the *measured native code-size reduction* after the inline/no-inline decision, `S(caller_before) − S(caller_after) + (callee size delta if the callee is deleted)`. For register allocation (their stated next target, not this paper's shipped result), the analogous reward is the block-frequency-weighted count of spill/reload moves introduced. **Sample efficiency is decisively asymmetric, and directly measured (Table 2):** PG reaches 4.95% size reduction in ~12h on a single 72-thread workstation; ES needs ~60h for a *worse* result (3.74%); a deeper ES network gets 5.94% but needs ~150h. Quoting: *"the key advantage of PG is that it has much higher sample efficiency than ES... over 20× computational resources are needed to train an ES policy of similar quality."*

**Deployment / determinism — the load-bearing quote for OSIL's own invariant, verbatim (§3.1, persona: "Compiler User"):**

> *"'Online' training — meaning, training while the compiler is executing in production — is an anti-goal for us: it would hurt determinism and compilation performance. Instead, policy training happens offline. Trained policies are embedded in the compiler as statically linked native code, and the resulting compiler is subjected to the same release process it currently is. Build and release infrastructures and pipelines of targets using the compiler do not need to be changed... Build determinism using the ML-enabled compiler is ensured because the policies are fixed - no training happens when the compiler runs, only inference. While native compilation doesn't guarantee timeliness, it eliminates one source of concern."*

This is a direct, primary-sourced, industrial-scale precedent for the exact question the task asks. MLGO's answer: **freeze the model, version it, ship it through the compiler's ordinary build/release pipeline as a plain artifact (an AOT-compiled TensorFlow SavedModel, compiled to a native `.o` and linked in — §5.2), and never let training or exploratory randomness reach the release build.** MLGO further names the split explicitly as two *modes*: **"release mode"** (fixed weights, zero runtime ML dependency, deterministic) versus **"development mode"** (dynamically loaded TFLite, used only for research/training, where *"exploration... is delegated to a TensorFlow mechanism that introduces some randomness in decisions... Care must be taken to remove such randomness before shipping and re-validate the model"*). This two-mode split is structurally identical to what `optimizer/` already has by convention: the default deterministic pick versus `--force-interleave` as an explicit, opt-in override for experimentation — `optimizer/` already has the right shape of escape hatch; it just doesn't yet have a "development mode" that trains anything.

**What MLGO's authors explicitly considered and rejected — and why it does *not* transfer to OSIL (important disanalogy, §3.2):**

> *"we do not see automated tuning of existing heuristic parameters as a viable solution. Tuning parameters have been available in compilers for a long time, and the experience has been that a set of values does not translate well from target to target. In addition, requiring re-tuning would complicate product build and release pipelines."*

Read carefully, this is **not** a general argument against calibrating constants — it is an argument specific to LLVM's inlining heuristic, which has *many* interacting, hand-tuned thresholds (call-site cost estimates, hotness bonuses, SIMD-instruction-percentage bonuses, deferral heuristics) with no direct physical referent, discovered by years of ad hoc tuning across a huge, poorly characterized feature space. `optimizer/`'s two constants are a different kind of object entirely: **named, physically-meaningful microarchitectural quantities** — a dependent-multiply latency in cycles, a throughput bound in lanes/cycle — that are independently measurable by microbenchmark on the actual target machine, the same way a roofline-model calibration measures peak FLOPs and memory bandwidth. MLGO rejected constant-tuning because the constants in *their* problem are not physical constants; OSIL's are. This distinction should be stated explicitly if this research is cited later, to prevent MLGO's rejection from being mis-applied here.

## 4. The determinism tension, synthesized

Applying §3 directly: **a frozen, versioned artifact is sufficient**, and is the field's own production-grade answer, not a workaround this project would be inventing alone. `optimizer/` already uses a comparable pattern elsewhere — `egg`'s `deterministic` feature is enabled specifically so tie-breaking in the e-graph never depends on hash-iteration order (README, "Known gaps"). The calibration ledger recommended in §6 below extends the same discipline one level up: the two constants become a versioned, checked-in file (not a runtime-learned or runtime-loaded value), so `main.rs` at any given commit is exactly as deterministic as it is today — it just reads two numbers from a file instead of a `const` in source, and that file's provenance is auditable.

**No prior art was found for "deterministic but *continuously* learned"** — MLGO's own solution is to *not* be continuously learned in production; training happens entirely offline, and the release artifact is frozen for the whole lifetime of a compiler release. This is worth stating plainly: the literature does not offer a way to have both continuous online learning and byte-determinism simultaneously — it offers *freeze-then-ship* as the resolution. **ASSUMPTION** (not independently verified in this pass): MLGO's paper does not itself claim the *neural network's floating-point inference* is bit-identical across different host CPUs/compilers — a subtlety that would matter for a genuinely learned model shipped cross-platform, but is moot for OSIL's recommendation, since §6 recommends fitting two `f64` scalars with an ordinary least-squares solver and writing them as literal decimal text into a checked-in file, which carries no floating-point-inference-reproducibility risk at all.

## 5. Analytical vs. learned, for THIS problem's actual shape — both sides argued

**The actual candidate space, checked against `optimizer/src/main.rs::rules()` and `optimizer/README.md`:** under the `reassociable` guard, the space is `Chain` plus `Lanes{w=4, i}` for `i ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}` — **5 candidates total**, width pinned by NEON's 128-bit register (not a free parameter), interleave the only true search dimension, and realistically bounded above by the architectural register file (there is no plausible reason to search past `i=16` or so — accumulator registers run out). Under the `exact` guard, the space collapses to 1 candidate (`Chain` only) — the constants don't even get exercised.

**The case FOR a learned model:** none of it currently applies, and stating that plainly is itself the finding. Every system surveyed built or adopted a learned model *because* its authors could name a search space that had outgrown exhaustive measurement — Halide: *"hundreds of thousands of potential schedules"* per beam-search pass (§6.4); AutoTVM/Ansor/TenSet: *"the search space is usually in the order of millions on CPUs and billions on GPUs"* (TenSet §3.3). In every one of these systems, a learned model exists to answer the question *"which of these many thousands of untested candidates is even worth measuring,"* because measurement is the bottleneck. If `optimizer/`'s realization space grows by orders of magnitude — more reduction operators, more vector widths across architectures, richer scheduling choices analogous to Halide's tiling/fusion grid — this argument reverses, and everything in §1–§2 becomes directly applicable at that point (Halide's hybrid hand-terms-plus-learned-coefficients architecture in particular, given `optimizer/`'s model is already shaped that way).

**The case FOR calibration (i.e., against a learned model, right now):** with 5 candidates, exhaustive measurement is not a scarce resource to conserve — it is what `optimizer/bench/Makefile` already does, in full, on every `make all` run (builds and times `Chain` and all four `Lanes` variants). A cost model whose only job is to *rank 5 already-cheaply-measurable candidates* is solving a strictly easier problem than any system surveyed, all of which must estimate performance for candidates *before* any of them can be measured. Injecting a learned model here adds real, unforced cost — feature engineering, training-data generation, a train/held-out split, model versioning and freezing, a retraining trigger, all the machinery §1–§3 describe — to eliminate a bottleneck that was never present. The `optimizer/` harness's actual job (per its own README: *"the harness checks the model's PICK against ground truth for this reason"*) is to validate a compile-time-only formula (the optimizer itself cannot shell out to a benchmark while running, unlike Ansor's runtime search loop) against measurements taken *offline*, once, ahead of time — which is exactly a calibration problem, not a search problem.

**Verdict:** the honest framing of "should we learn a cost model" for this specific object is a category error at the current scale — the right question is "how does a two-constant, compile-time-only formula get its two constants right, on this machine, with evidence." That is calibration. Revisit this verdict if and when the candidate space genuinely grows past what `make all` can exhaustively time in a reasonable CI budget (a concrete, checkable trigger — proposed as the recalibration-vs-relearn threshold in §6.6).

## 6. Autotuning frameworks reusable off the shelf

Checked live, 2026-08-24:

- **OpenTuner** (Ansel et al., PACT 2014). `jansel/opentuner` on GitHub: last commit **2026-08-15** (9 days before this research), 438 stars, 71 open issues — actively fielding issues, genuinely alive. PyPI package `opentuner`, latest **0.8.8**, released 2022-04-11 — the PyPI release cadence lags GitHub HEAD by ~4 years; a `pip install opentuner` today would not track recent fixes. **Architecturally the right shape of tool** for "calibrate N parameters against a measured objective" — an ensemble of search techniques (differential evolution, AUC-bandit technique selection, Nelder-Mead) driven by a user-supplied `MeasurementInterface`. **But it is overkill here**: fitting 2 continuous scalars against ~20–40 measured (kind, n) pairs is a textbook least-squares problem with a closed-form/one-shot solution; standing up a genetic-search framework to do it adds a Python dependency to a Rust project's build for no accuracy benefit. **Directly relevant cautionary precedent, read in Adams et al. 2019 §2:** *"A simpler version of this approach was reimplemented in the OpenTuner framework [Ansel et al. 2014], but it can only effectively schedule the simplest pipelines [Mullapudi et al. 2015]"* — this exact lineage tried OpenTuner's generic black-box search as a Halide-scheduling substitute and found it insufficiently domain-aware. That is not evidence against using OpenTuner for a much narrower 2-scalar curve fit; it *is* evidence that OpenTuner is not a substitute for domain modeling when domain modeling (i.e., the analytical formula `optimizer/` already has) is available and correct in form.
- **KernelTuner** (`KernelTuner/kernel_tuner` org on GitHub): last commit **2026-08-21** (3 days before this research), latest release **1.5.0** published the same day — actively, currently maintained. Purpose-built for GPU/CPU **kernel launch-parameter** search (block size, tile size, unroll factor) against measured runtime — closer in spirit to what `bench/Makefile` already does by hand than to cost-model calibration. Could be pointed at the constant-fitting problem by wrapping the analytical formula as the "kernel" under test, but that is an unnatural fit (KernelTuner tunes discrete launch configs against measured runtime directly, not continuous regression coefficients against prediction error) — not recommended over a direct least-squares fit.
- **ATLAS-style empirical BLAS tuning:** referenced only as historical precedent inside AutoTVM's own related work (*"Black box optimization (auto-tuning) is used in high-performance computing libraries such as ATLAS and FFTW"*) — architecturally the closest historical analogue to "measure this machine's real constants at build/install time," but **not independently verified for current maintenance status in this pass. ASSUMPTION-flagged as unresearched**, not recommended or rejected.

**Verdict:** none of these frameworks earns its dependency weight for a 2-parameter calibration problem against a handful of existing measured data points. The existing `bench/Makefile` harness plus a small standalone least-squares fit script (Rust, or a `uv run` Python script per this repo's stated tooling convention) is sufficient, keeps the calibration step inside the project's own toolchain, and avoids importing a genetic-search framework as a build dependency of a Rust compiler component.

## 7. Overfitting defenses in compiler autotuning, synthesized across all four systems

Common thread, all primary-sourced above, converging from four independently developed systems:

1. **The strong form of held-out is a whole unseen kernel/network/DAG, not a random slice of one already-seen kernel's variants.** Halide: disjoint *generating processes* (synthetic-random for training, curated real applications for evaluation — never mixed). TenSet: an entire named network (ResNet-50, or in other experiments MobileNet-V2/ResNeXt-50/BERT-tiny/BERT-base) excluded wholesale from training.
2. **Rank/pick accuracy is the metric that matters; regression accuracy (RMSE, R²) can be actively misleading.** Three independent convergent findings: Halide minimizes L2 error on *throughput* specifically to focus model capacity on distinguishing *fast* schedules from each other rather than accurately predicting how bad the bad ones are (§4.2); AutoTVM finds pairwise rank loss matches-or-beats MSE regression loss on every workload tested (§3.2, Fig. 5); TenSet directly documents a model with excellent RMSE/R² choosing *worse* real candidates than a model with terrible RMSE/R² trained on a ranking loss instead (§5.1, Table 3) — concluding in their own words, *"the problem we are tackling is fundamentally a ranking problem rather than a regression problem."*
3. **Transfer across hardware is real but degrades, and the degradation is measured, not silently absorbed.** TenSet §5.4: cross-platform transfer beats cold start but underperforms same-platform training. Adams et al. §6.2: an under-sampled ARM model still wins, but by a much smaller margin than the well-sampled x86 model. Neither system hides this; both report it as a number.
4. **A dataset/model's own authors flag its own domain narrowness.** TenSet §7: floating-point, dense-network subgraphs only, partitioned by one specific algorithm — the authors' own limitation, not an outside critique.

**Directly applicable to `optimizer/`:** the constants currently in `main.rs` are "M-series ballpark, not measured on this M4" — exactly TenSet's/Adams et al.'s named failure mode (an unvalidated cross-hardware transfer, one step further removed since there was never even a source-hardware measurement, only an unstated prior belief) — which `optimizer/README.md`'s own "Known gaps" section already names as the reason the harness checks the model's pick against ground truth. This research confirms that instinct is exactly right, and gives it a name and a design pattern (§6 below) rather than leaving it as a standing caveat.

## RECOMMENDED LOOP DESIGN (concrete)

**Headline answer to the task's explicit fallback question: yes, the honest recommendation is "do not learn a model yet; measure the two constants directly," and it is defended above (§5) on the literature's own terms, not by default.**

### 6.1 What is measured

For every kernel case file under `optimizer/cases/` (currently `s312.osil`, `s312-exact.osil`, `s317.osil`, `s317-noclosed.osil`, `s352.osil` — three structurally distinct reduction shapes: product-over-array, product-over-repeated-scalar, and dot-product-via-`zip`) and every realization `osil-opt` licenses for it (`Chain`, and `Lanes{4,i}` for each `i` admitted under the `reassociable` guard), record what `bench/Makefile` already produces on `make all`: the built C variant's measured wall-clock (min or median of repeated trials, matching the existing `bench`/`accuracy` harness convention), **plus** the model's *currently configured* predicted cost for the same `(kind, n)` pair. Note explicitly: `s312-exact` (guard `numeric_semantics = exact`) only ever exercises `Chain`, so it contributes a latency-only data point, never a throughput one; closed-form realizations (if/when wired into the rule set for `s317`'s `closed_form = permitted` guard) don't invoke `MUL_LATENCY`/`LANES_PER_CYCLE` at all and are out of scope for this calibration entirely.

### 6.2 Ledger format on disk

Two files, both under a new `optimizer/calibration/` directory, both text, both diff-legible, matching this repo's existing append-only/plain-text conventions (corpus files, ADRs):

**`optimizer/calibration/measurements.jsonl`** — append-only, one JSON object per measured `(kernel, realization, machine)` row, never rewritten:
```json
{"date":"2026-08-24","kernel":"s312","op":"mul","extent":32000,"kind":"chain","measured_ns":512340,"machine":"Apple-M4","cc":"clang-17","cflags":"-O3 -march=native","git_sha":"<sha>"}
{"date":"2026-08-24","kernel":"s312","op":"mul","extent":32000,"kind":"lanes","w":4,"i":2,"measured_ns":68210,"machine":"Apple-M4","cc":"clang-17","cflags":"-O3 -march=native","git_sha":"<sha>"}
```

**`optimizer/calibration/constants.toml`** — the *only* file that changes what `main.rs` uses at run time; `main.rs` reads these two values instead of hardcoding `const MUL_LATENCY: f64 = 4.0` / `LANES_PER_CYCLE: f64 = 8.0`:
```toml
# Calibrated 2026-08-24 on Apple M4, clang 17 -O3 -march=native.
# Fit by optimizer/calibration/fit.py against measurements.jsonl rows
# tagged machine = "Apple-M4". Re-run and bump version on any
# re-measurement (new machine, new compiler, new OS).
version = "v1"
mul_latency_cycles = 3.8       # was: unmeasured constant 4.0 in main.rs (ASSUMPTION, now retired)
lanes_per_cycle    = 7.6       # was: unmeasured constant 8.0 in main.rs (ASSUMPTION, now retired)
machine   = "Apple-M4"
fit_train = ["s312", "s317-noclosed"]
fit_heldout = ["s352"]
fit_r2 = 0.94
pick_correctness = "PASS: s352 held-out pick matches measured-fastest"
```
This is the entire "self-improvement" surface — a calibration event revises exactly these two scalars plus their provenance fields, never the formula in `model()`. This mirrors ADR-0009's "guard-gated rewrites are DATA, not code" one level up: the two constants are the data; `model()`'s `max(latency, throughput) + combine` formula stays hand-written and auditable as code.

**`optimizer/calibration/HISTORY.md`** — one append-only line per calibration event: date, constants version, which kernels were held out, PASS/FAIL on the pick-correctness gate, R². This is the ledger's audit trail — the concrete instantiation of "revisions append-only with the evidence that forced them" from the standing rubric-ledger philosophy.

### 6.3 What gets revised — and how the fit actually runs

Not a training loop. The model is `latency.max(throughput) + combine`, piecewise-linear in the two constants for a fixed set of measured `(kind, n)` rows — an ordinary nonlinear least-squares fit (`scipy.optimize.least_squares`, or an equivalent small Rust crate) against `measurements.jsonl`, minimizing `Σ (modeled_cost(constants) − measured_ns)²` **weighted toward the fast candidates**, exactly matching the convergent design principle from §7.2 (Halide's throughput-weighted loss, Ansor's `w_p = y` weighting) — cheap to implement here (weight = `1/measured_ns`, or equivalently fit on log-throughput) and directly reuses a finding independently reached by two unrelated groups rather than inventing a new weighting scheme. **This has no "stopping criterion" in the ML-training sense** — 2 parameters, closed-form/one-shot solve, deterministic given the input data, no hyperparameters, no risk of the optimizer itself overfitting mid-training. This absence of a training loop is itself part of the argument in §5: if the fit needed early-stopping or a learning-rate schedule, that would be evidence the problem had outgrown calibration.

### 6.4 Microbenchmark calibration — reusing what `bench/` already measures

The task explicitly raises measuring the two constants directly rather than fitting them indirectly. Concretely, and with no new benchmark code required for a first cut: the **`Chain`** measurement *is* a latency-bound microbenchmark already (`n` strictly sequential dependent multiplies — exactly the textbook dependent-chain-latency microbenchmark pattern), and the **`Lanes{4,8}`** measurement (highest interleave currently generated) is already close to a throughput-bound microbenchmark (enough independent accumulator chains that the throughput term should dominate the latency term in `model()`'s own `max()`). The two existing extreme measurements are, in effect, already the two calibration probes an HPC roofline-style calibration would design from scratch (**ASSUMPTION**, offered as a pointer and not independently verified in this pass: this connects to the established empirical-roofline-calibration literature — e.g., the Empirical Roofline Toolkit / `likwid-bench` style of directly microbenchmarking peak latency and peak throughput on the target machine — a well-established sibling practice to what tensor-compiler cost-model papers do, but a different literature that this pass did not separately research). Practically: `fit.py` can and should use *all* measured rows (not just the two extremes) in the weighted least-squares fit — more data points only tighten the fit — but the fact that the two extremes alone would already roughly triangulate both constants is a useful sanity check and a good "does the fit look right" first pass.

### 6.5 Held-out set

With three structurally distinct kernel families today (`s312`: product-reduction over an array; `s317`: product-reduction over a repeated scalar; `s352`: dot-product via `zip`), apply TenSet's strong-form protocol directly: **fit on `s312` + `s317-noclosed` (the two families that exercise `Lanes` under the `reassociable` guard), hold out `s352` entirely** — never used to fit the constants, only to check the resulting model's *pick* against `s352`'s own measured-fastest realization. As more TSVC_2 kernels are added to `cases/` (151 were surveyed per `README.md`; only a handful are in the corpus so far), the held-out set should grow *faster* than the training set — fitting 2 scalars needs very few points; confidence that the formula generalizes across kernel shapes needs breadth. `s312-exact` (guard `exact`, `Chain`-only) is a free bonus latency-only data point usable in either split, since it carries no `Lanes` information either way.

### 6.6 Stopping / regression criterion

Two gates, deliberately not one — directly implementing §7.2's convergent "rank/pick accuracy over regression accuracy" finding rather than gating on R² alone:

- **Pick-correctness gate (primary, blocking).** For every held-out kernel, the realization `model()` ranks first must equal the realization with the lowest *measured* wall-clock time. Binary, per-kernel, machine-checkable — the same shape as this repo's existing negative-fixture conventions (`conformance/rejections/R00x`, `conformance/equivalence/ES00x`). A calibration event that improves the fit on the training kernels but flips this to FAIL on any held-out kernel is a **regression** and must not be promoted — this is the concrete, mechanical form of "never score on what you optimized" for this specific rubric. `optimizer/`'s own harness already does half of this today (checks the model's current, unfitted pick against ground truth on one kernel); this generalizes it to every held-out kernel, run on every calibration event, not just once.
- **Prediction-quality gate (secondary, informational, non-blocking).** Track R² (or Spearman rank correlation, arguably more honest per TenSet's own finding that R² can mislead — worth computing both) across all measured rows in `HISTORY.md` over time. Never gate promotion on this number alone — TenSet's Table 3 is direct, primary-sourced evidence that a model can have excellent R² and a worse real-world pick than a model with terrible R² trained toward ranking. Its purpose is trend-watching (is the fit degrading over many calibration events — an early-warning signal for "the analytical formula's *form*, not just its constants, may now be wrong"), not gating.

**When to recalibrate, not continuously:** MLGO's own explicit stance — *"we do not want to retrain policies too frequently (it is an adoption blocker)"* — and its own measured finding that policy effectiveness *"degrade[s] somewhat"* after 4 months of independent software/compiler drift (§6.2.2, Fig. 6) — both argue for **event-triggered, not continuous, recalibration**: a new machine, a new compiler version, a new OS, or a detected pick-correctness failure on an existing held-out kernel, each logged as one `HISTORY.md` line, not a background job.

### 6.7 Where this lives, and where it explicitly does not

`optimizer/` is `README.md`-declared **PROBE** status — "not gated, not in `just test`." Recommend `optimizer/calibration/` stays a standalone script + ledger for now, invoked manually or via a new `make calibrate` target in `bench/Makefile` (after `make all`, appending to `measurements.jsonl`), **not** wired into the repo's gated `just test` loop yet — consistent with the optimizer's own declared probe status, and revisited only if/when the optimizer graduates out of PROBE.

## Validity & limitations

**Valid as of:** 2026-08-24. Re-evaluate if:
- `optimizer/`'s candidate space grows past what `make all` can exhaustively measure in a reasonable CI budget (the concrete trigger named in §5/§6.6 for revisiting "calibration vs. learned model").
- New scheduling dimensions are added that are not reducible to two named physical constants (at that point, Halide's hand-terms-plus-learned-coefficients architecture, §1.2, becomes the directly applicable precedent, not a hypothetical one).
- A future TenSet-scale dataset appears with actual Apple-Silicon NEON coverage (none was found in this pass — TenSet's ARM entry is server-class Graviton2, not M-series).

**Limitations:** this research is bounded by the five primary sources read in full plus live infrastructure checks; it did not exhaustively survey the broader ML-for-compilers literature (e.g., the several 2025–2026 preprints on cross-hardware transfer surfaced by search but not read in full — COGNATE, TLP, Moses — noted only as corroborating titles, not cited as evidence). The scale-mismatch argument in §5 is a judgment call about *this* project's *current* candidate space, stated as such, not a general claim that analytical calibration always beats learned models.

---

**Epistemological note:** this research represents the best available primary-sourced evidence as of 2026-08-24. The recommendation not to build a learned model is a claim about *fit to current scale*, not a claim that learned cost models are wrong in general — §1–§3 document, in the surveyed systems' own words, exactly the conditions under which they became the right tool. Re-read §5 before deciding whether those conditions now hold.
